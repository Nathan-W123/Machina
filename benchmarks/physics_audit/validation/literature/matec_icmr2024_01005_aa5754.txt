Single-Point Incremental Forming (SPIF) of 
AA5754 Aluminium Alloy Blanks: experimental 
and numerical studies 
Yuhai Jin1,2, Xiangnan Yu1,2, Heli Liu1,2, Denis J. Politis3, Liliang Wang1,2,* 
1 Department of Mechanical Engineering, Imperial College London, London, SW7 2AZ, UK 
2 SmartForming Research Base, Imperial College London, London, SW7 2AZ, UK  
3Department of Mechanical and Manufacturing Engineering, University of Cyprus, Nicosia, 
1678, Cyprus 
Abstract: This study presents a Finite Element (FE) simulation model for 
Single-Point Incremental Forming (SPIF) of a panel  part using AA5754 
Aluminium Alloy . The  precision of model was highly enhanced  through 
experimental data integration by uniaxial tensile tests (UATT) to determine 
material flow stress and anisotropic properties. The Voce model accurately 
predicted material flow stress within a 5% error margin, and equations were 
concluded to convert Lankford coefficients (R-values) into anisotropic stress 
ratios for Abaqus/Explicit simulation.  Experimental validation 
demonstrated a close agreement in major strain and thickness distribution, 
with a maximum error below 5.8% across high and low forming speeds. 
 
Keywords: Single-point increment al forming (SPIF); Uniaxial tensile test (UATT); Sheet metal 
forming; Finite element analysis (FEA). 
1 Introduction 
Single point incremental forming (SPIF)  is characterized as a dieless metal forming 
technology[1], where sheet metal s undergo incremental transformation to their  definitive 
geometry via successive, minor, localized deformations [2,3]. Recognized for its innovative 
approach to deformation , SPIF is acclaimed for enhancing formability, low-cost, and 
operational flexibility. Localized deformation within a small plastic zone  and incremental 
nature were considered as the distinctive features of this process, which contributes to 
elevated material formability, enabling forming process on metals that were traditionally 
unprioritized due to their limited formability[2,4]. Flexibility is another advantage of SPIF, 
facilitated by Computer Numerical Control (CNC) machine s, e nabling the precise 
customization of tool paths to achieve desired  shape while simultaneously ensuring 
geometric accuracy[5]. From a cost perspective, the dieless nature presents a cost-effective 
alternative to conventional metal forming techniques such as stamping[6] and deep 
drawing[7] in low batch size manufacturing, of typically fewer than 250 parts for larger 
components and 700 for smaller ones [8,9]. Low-cost tooling[10] is another advantage of

2 Methodology 
2.1 Uniaxial Tensile Test (UATT) of AA5754 Blanks 
An Aluminium Alloy 5754 (AA5754) sheet with a thickness of 1.5 mm was selected as the 
testing specimen. Two uniaxial tensile tests (UATT) were conducted at room temperature 
under different strain rates, 0.1/s and 0.001/s respectively. The specimen was securely fixed 
to the grips of an Instron machine parallel to the sensor of Digital Image Correlation (DIC) 
system for capturing the material deformation  (Fig. 1a). The surface of each specimen was 
treated with two different types of sprays to create a stochastic patter n (Fig. 1b) for strain 
analysis facilitated by the DIC system (Fig. 1c and 1d).  
The stress data obtained from the Instron machine was integrated with strain data to plot 
the stress-strain relationship from UATT experiments (Fig. 1f). Strain rate exhibits minimal 
influence on the flow stress of AA5754 at room temperature, with the stress observed at the 
lower strain rate (0.001/s) marginally exceeding that at the higher strain rate (0.1/s ). This 
phenomenon was caused by dynamic aging  resulting from the interaction between 
dislocations and solute atoms [15,16]. The Voce model represented in Equation 1 was 
calibrated to describe the plastic flow stress behaviour . It demonstrated a strong alignment 
with the stress-strain curve derived from the UATT experiments, with an error no more than 
5%. The degree of precision validated the effectiveness of the model accuracy in describing 
the generalized deformation behaviour of the AA5754.

2.2 Single point incremental forming (SPIF) of AA5754 Blanks 
In this study, the manufacturing target was a truncated cone, specified by an outer diameter 
Do of 150 mm, an inner diameter Di of 50 mm, and a vertical height of 50 mm. This geometry 
was manufactured from 195 mm x 195 mm square sheet of AA5754 aluminium alloy using 
SPIF under two different forming speeds. The high forming speed experiment had a feed rate 
of 3000 mm/min and a radi al incremental step of 2 mm, and the low forming speed 
experiment had a feed rate of 900 mm/min and a radial incremental step of 0.5 mm. The high-
speed steel forming tool, with a hemispherical head of diameter 12.7 mm and surface 
roughness Ra = 0.4, was inserted into a CNC milling machine to perform the process at a 
constant spindle speed of 500 RPM. The clamping fixture held the sheet at a fixed position 
(Fig. 2a) for the CNC machine to perform any desired tool path programmed by G -code, 
specifically a spiral in this case. 
The electrolytic etching was used to create a regular grid pattern for the DIC system to 
measure strain behaviour, characterized by nodes with a diameter of 1 mm and spaced 1 mm 
apart. The distribution of surface major strain and thickness of test specimens were measured 
using a GOM -ARGUS photogrammetry system. Approximately 150 -200 images were 
captured from multiple angles by adjusting the height of the camera and rotating the turntable, 
on which the specimen was mounted, through a full 3 60°  rotation, as illustrated in Fig. 2b. 
The turntable was enclosed with 4 rectangular scale bars, each featuring 3 calibration squares, 
total 12 calibration points to ensure precise spatial referencing and accuracy in the 
measurement process.

2.3 FE model setup 
In the Abaqus/Explicit simulation setup, an analytical rigid forming tool was assembled with 
a 3D deformable she ll featured with embedded material card with experimental flow stress 
behaviour and anisotropic properties . To enhance the precision of the simulation and to 
comprehensively account for the effects of bending and stretching on the material, the model 
incorporated five elements in the thickness direction of the blank[19]. The simulation model 
had a total of 8464 elements classified as S4R type. In the forming region, a mesh grid with 
2 mm element size was employed to ensure precise capture of deformation behaviour. A 
larger mesh size was applied in the clamping area which has all degrees of freedom restricted, 
optimizing computational efficiency without compromising the accuracy of the simulation 
results in areas critical to the analysis (Fig. 3a). The interaction between the forming tool and 
the sheet was governed by the penalty contact method  with Coulomb's friction law  
implemented. Coefficient of friction (CoF) was assumed to be 0.2 considering lubrication 
was used in the SPIF process. The post-form truncated cone was simulated in Fig. 3b.

3 Results and Discussion 
Fig. 4a shows the major strain distribution obtained in FE simulation results , a nodal path  
along the diagonal was established for data extraction , which provides the representative 
characteristics of the overall forming behaviours . For experimental DIC post-processing 
analysis, images captured from an angle that displayed the maximum number of identifiable 
grid points were selected to improve the accuracy of the results  (Fig. 4b). Furthermore, 3 
cross-sections positioned along the diagonal and spaced equally at 1  mm intervals were 
measured and averaged to enhance the reliability of the data (Fig. 4c). 
The thickness and major strain distribution along the diagonal curvilinear length of the 
final truncated cone product by SPIF manufacturing were compared to outcomes derived 
from Abaqus simulation, which embedded a material model integrating Voce hardening law 
and anisotropic constants. The comparisons of SPIF at a fast forming speed (3000 mm/min

4 Conclusions 
In this work, a finite element (FE) model was developed for the single point incremental 
forming (SPIF) process of AA5754 blanks. Embedded with a comprehensive material card 
derived from experimental data, the FE model generated results reached close agreement with 
the experimental outcomes, regarding blank major strain and thickness distribution.  
(1) Uni -axial Tensile Test (UATT) experiment s were performed on AA5754 -H111 
aluminium alloy at room temperature to characterize flow stress and anisotropic behaviours. 
The Voce model closely aligned with the UATT results with an error of less than 5%. 
(2) A method to convert Lankford coefficients to Abaqus embedded anisotropic stress 
ratio 𝑅𝑖𝑗 was recorded, specifically tailored for sheet metal forming simulations. 
(3) A comprehensive material card incorporating both plastic flow stress and anisotropic 
properties was developed and integrated into the FE model. This implementation 
significantly improved the accuracy of the simulation results, with a maximum error of no 
more than 5.8%.
